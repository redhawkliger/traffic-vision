Panel disclosure

The five "experts" are author-constructed research lenses, not independent domain authorities. Agreement across lenses is a strong hypothesis, not consensus of the field. Where all five converge, that is noted as such — it still requires the primary-source check.

The 60-second summary

Settled: vehicle detection on traffic video is good enough. What is not solved — and has never been independently validated — is FHWA 13-class classification from a fixed overhead camera. The geometry that makes turning movements countable is the same geometry that hides axles, and FHWA's classes are defined by axle count and spacing. Deployed systems therefore ship 6 classes or truck groups, never 13.

Contested: what the published accuracy numbers mean. Headline "95%+" figures appear to be per-site, vendor-reported, on collapsed class schemes, with human QC inside the number. The only large independent multi-vendor validation found error spanning more than an order of magnitude across vendors and sites.

The actionable asymmetry: three lenses independently conclude that the missing artifact in this industry is not a better detector but a published, audited, per-interval accuracy figure with a class confusion matrix — and that incumbents are structurally unable to supply one, because their installed base is sold on an unaudited number.

1. Full FHWA-13 classification from video is unachieved — but not for the reason all five lenses gave

What is verified: Chen et al. (J. Computing in Civil Engineering 39(3)) state that no vision-based method has reached desired accuracy across all 13 classes — "primarily due to the interclass similarity issue, particularly among trucks." Vendors who publish at all publish 6 classes or truck groups.

Correction — "axles are unobservable from video" is overstated, and all five lenses asserted it. The paper cited as evidence for that claim does not name unobservable axles as the cause. It extracts axle configuration from surveillance video — its title is "Automated FHWA Vehicle Classification Using Combined Semantic and Geometric Features Extracted from Surveillance Videos." The barrier it identifies is interclass similarity, not missing information.

This is the clearest case in the briefing of lens convergence being wrong. Five independent lenses agreed on a mechanism; the primary source contradicts it. It also partially rehabilitates geometric feature extraction, which two lenses dismissed.

A second, harder ceiling is confirmed: rare classes are sample-starved even with axle sensors. ODOT FHWA-OK-14-17 recorded class 7 n=42, class 11 n=47, class 12 n=64 out of 20,099 vehicles. That is a ground-truth labelling ceiling that bounds any supervised vision model — you cannot train or validate what you cannot sample.

2. Independent validation shows an order-of-magnitude error spread

Verified verbatim. NCHRP Web-Only Document 436 (Project 03-144, Tsapakis et al., TTI, 2025): motorized volumes from video-based systems "varied significantly (WMAPE = 1.4% − 33.7%) by vendor, equipment model, and intersection"; non-motorized 3.6%–93.7%, and "most video-based systems undercounted non-motorized users." The 3.6% best case required two dedicated cameras on opposite corners, extended calibration and high mounting — the good number is an artifact of effort, not algorithm.

Separately, inductive loop detectors in the same document scored WMAPE 4.0%–45.5%. Different modality, different chapter — no contradiction. A hybrid V2X deployment at four Phoenix intersections scored 0.6%–4.2%.

Two author-stated limits that matter directly here: accuracy degrades as volume rises, and turning-movement counts are less accurate than through movements. The deliverable this project is aiming at is the harder one.

Confidence reduced 8→7: this is a Web-Only Document, which its own front matter states "has not been subjected to the formal TRB peer review process." Cite as practitioner guidance, not peer-reviewed literature. Occlusion is one listed cause among several, not the named primary failure mode as originally reported.

Correction — the UDOT "20-point spread" was over-framed. The figures (96.4% Traficon / 92.0% Autoscope / 85.2% Iteris / 75.8% Peek) are verbatim from Martin, Dharmavaram & Stevanovic, Evaluation of UDOT's Video Detection Systems, MPC-04-166 — but it dates from December 2004, it is not independent of UDOT (it evaluates UDOT's own installed systems, with a UDOT advisory committee and UDOT-supplied Peek hardware), and the spread is confounded by unequal testing: Peek 4 sites, Iteris 2, Autoscope 1, Traficon 1 — 8 intersections total. The authors state plainly: "These results cannot be generalized because of the difference in the numbers of locations tested." The claim that the four vendors published "similar spec-sheet claims" appears nowhere in the report — that was lens editorialising, and I repeated it.

Precision on the weather figures: 87.2% was day and dusk measured separately; the 81.3% "inclement weather" figure is specifically the snow condition. Night was 73.4% with 19.9% false calls.

3. Production counting does not depend on unbroken tracking

Published tracking performance is far weaker than headline MOTA figures suggest — best PR-MOTA on UA-DETRAC reportedly ~21–24%, and the AI City Challenge's 0.9389 headline is 70% weighted on efficiency, not accuracy. Production systems resolve this architecturally: project to the ground plane, then count directional tripwire crossings per approach, paired into OD zone pairs. A stopped vehicle never crosses; detector flicker cannot fabricate a crossing; fragmentation becomes largely irrelevant.

6. The reference standard itself was off by 24.5% — verified verbatim

In Flock's study, the Caltrans inductive loop detectors used as the comparison baseline overcounted by ~24.5% — "induction loop detectors reported a total of 3,392 vehicles, representing an overcount of approximately 24.5% relative to the reference observations" (3,392 / 2,724 = 1.245). 8. The standards are softer than they look — and one key counter-claim was misattributed

VDOT VTRC 26-R53 — confirmed verbatim. A stakeholder survey "established a 5% error threshold"; a "formal eight-step 'fail-fast' protocol" runs from 24-hour volumetric screening to classification under adverse conditions; continuous count stations are "calibrat[ed]… against absolute, human-verified video ground truth," at a stated $321,300 per device certification request. Note the method: they certify against human-verified video, which is exactly the approach this project adopted.

FHWA TMG is advisory, not a standard. The "10% by class" bar is a should-level recommendation (§3.2.3, p. 3-15: algorithms should "correctly classify all vehicle types on their roadways (within 10% of the counting tolerances, bias, and error by class)"). FHWA Notice N 5600.7, which transmitted the first TMG in 1985, states it is "an advisory document… not to be considered a Federal Standard."

Correction — "manual counts carry 4–5% classification error by FHWA's own accounting" is FALSE as attributed, and the figure itself is contested. No FHWA document states it; the 2022 TMG treats manual error only qualitatively. The numbers originate in Zheng & McDonald (2012), a UK national-census study (<1% total count error, 4–5% classification error). And Majumder & Wilmot (2023) re-measured and obtained 1.05% total / 1.08% classification error.

This materially rescues the "objective truth" objective. The skeptic's strongest argument against it — that human counting is too error-prone to serve as a reference — rests on a misattributed and contested figure. Careful human video counting may be an order of magnitude better than that claim implied.

ASTM E1318-09(2024) defines four WIM types with per-application tolerances. The nuance matters: Type IV is specified for weight enforcement — it is simply "not approved for use in the United States," which is why static scales retain the legal role. Not quite the clean "WIM never writes citations" story.

The hidden connection

Three lenses describe one causal chain without any of them seeing the whole of it.

The academic shows tracking is genuinely weak (~24% PR-MOTA). The practitioner shows production systems sidestep tracking entirely via tripwire crossings and OD pairing. The historian shows video detection never received an ASTM-style validation protocol, and the economist shows why nobody has an incentive to publish one.

Put together: the single most important architectural fact in this field — that you must count crossings, not tracks — is not widely documented because there is no forcing function to document it. The absence of a validation standard didn't just hide bad accuracy; it suppressed the circulation of the design knowledge that produces good accuracy. A newcomer reconstructs it by failing, as this project did.

Key assumption · the missing sixth lens

No lens addressed the camera owner. All five treat the camera as a given. None examined the agency that owns it — its retention policy, its liability posture, its willingness to let a third party build a commercial product on a public feed.

The skeptic brushed this, and verification both confirmed and constrained it. FHWA-HOP-16-033 Ch. 7 records verbatim that Oregon DOT "CCTV data will generally not be recorded or archived… Recorded images are considered public information and can be used as evidence," and that NYSDOT retains recordings "only for the minimum possible time."

But the inference is mine, not the source's. That document is a best-practices survey of TMC recording policy; it never states public-records liability as the driver, and it never addresses the short rolling window on public-facing feeds. The honest phrasing is that agency practice is consistent with a policy-driven no-archive norm — not that FHWA documents one. It still reframes the terms-of-use question from "is it allowed" to "does the owner have an active reason to prevent it," and a sixth lens — the public-sector data steward — could change the conclusions about using public feeds at all.

Actionable insight

1. Count crossings, not tracks — architecturally, now

This project measured 2.01× count inflation from track fragmentation and one vehicle resolving into nine track IDs. Tripwire crossings paired into OD zones make that failure mode largely moot. This is a redesign, not a tuning exercise, and it should precede any further work on the tracker.

2. Treat geometric length classification as unproven, and test it narrowly

Two lenses challenge it directly: projected length error is dominated by the bounding-box bottom edge (wrong by the vehicle's height), trailer gaps read as background, and turning vehicles break the vanishing-point assumption. Homography is validated for speed (Sochor et al., ~1.10 km/h mean error), not for length-based class. However, the published failures concern Class 5 vs Class 8 — a within-HV distinction. A Car/HV split at ~22 ft is far coarser. Test that specific boundary before adopting or discarding the method.

3. Validate per 15-minute interval, signed, with a confusion matrix

Counting accuracy is the difference of two large error streams, so a weak tracker can report 99% volume accuracy purely by cancellation. Daily or period totals hide this. Signed per-interval movement error plus a class confusion matrix is the only honest form — and it is what no incumbent publishes.

4. Build the audit loop before tuning the detector

A camera that passed validation last month fails after a PTZ nudge, a lens film, or a seasonal sun angle, and nothing in the pipeline announces it. Drift monitoring and a review queue are the deliverable, not an afterthought. Agency QA reportedly rejects counts for unexplainable approach imbalance and non-conserving corridor flow far more often than for a wrong truck class.

5. Do not train on the public datasets without a licence review

The commercially usable corpus is close to empty. Own-collected, own-labelled data is not merely a moat — on current reading it may be the only lawful option.

The frontier question

If no public dataset carries axle-level FHWA truth, and the incumbent reference standards themselves carry large error, what does "objective truth" actually mean for vehicle classification — and is a carefully human-counted video a sufficient reference, or does a defensible claim require a co-located axle sensor?

This is the question that decides whether the stated objective is reachable as framed. For volumes, a carefully counted video is plausibly complete truth — the video records everything that passed. For classification, the reference may need to be physical.

Verification shifted this toward "reachable." The strongest argument against human-counted video as a reference was a 4–5% manual classification error attributed to FHWA — which turns out to be misattributed to a contested UK study, with a 2023 re-measurement at ~1.1%. And VDOT's own 2026 acceptance framework certifies devices against "absolute, human-verified video ground truth." The method this project chose is the method a state DOT uses.
